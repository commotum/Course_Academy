# Guide to Gathering Course Information

## Purpose

Use this guide to find, verify, and organize the source material needed to create a course map for an existing university course. Begin with the university and course code, then gather the official description, syllabus, learning outcomes, and other relevant course materials.

This is the first stage of a two-stage process:

1. **Gather course information.** Establish what the course covers, its intended level, and what students are expected to learn. Produce a source-backed research brief for each course.
2. **Create the course map.** Use the research brief with `Guide_to_Creating_a_Course_Map.md` and the course-map template to write the Course Description, Course Overview, and Course Outcomes.

The research brief is not the finished course map. Its purpose is to preserve useful information, its sources, and any limitations before the writing stage begins.

## 1. Identify the Course

Start with the university’s name and the complete course code, including any prefix or suffix. Confirm the official course title rather than relying on a title supplied from memory or inferred from the code.

Record the term or academic year, campus, delivery mode, and section when specified. Establish whether the goal is to describe a particular offering or the course more generally.

For a particular offering, prioritize materials from that term and section. For a general map, begin with the current official catalog and use the closest relevant syllabus to add detail. Label the dates of older materials rather than presenting them as current requirements.

Check for former course numbers, renamed courses, cross-listings, and honors variants when relevant. Search confirmed alternate numbers as well, but do not assume that similar titles or codes establish equivalence. Record the university source that confirms any relationship.

When researching several courses, keep a separate record for each. Do not transfer a topic or outcome from one course to another simply because they belong to the same sequence.

## 2. Find the Official Course Description

Locate the course in the university’s official catalog or another official course listing. Confirm that the page belongs to the correct institution and catalog year.

Capture the official title, course code, credits, description, and any stated prerequisites, corequisites, or restrictions relevant to understanding the course. Preserve qualifications such as “introduction to,” “selected topics,” or “applications to”; they help establish the intended scope and depth.

Save the source URL and catalog year with the information. Distinguish quoted wording from a paraphrase so the later writing stage can tell which is which.

Use the description to establish the course’s broad identity and scope. Do not expand a brief catalog entry into a detailed topic list or a set of learning outcomes without additional supporting material.

## 3. Find Syllabi and Supporting Materials

Search official university sources first: department pages, instructor pages, course websites, syllabus repositories, and online-learning course listings. Follow links to attached syllabi, learning objectives, schedules, and related course documents.

Example searches include:

```text
"[University name]" "[Course code]" course description
site:[university domain] "[Course code]" syllabus
site:[university domain] "[Course code]" filetype:pdf
site:[university domain] "[Course code]" "learning outcomes"
```

Replace the bracketed text with the actual identifiers. Try spacing variations, confirmed former numbers, the course title, or a relevant term when the initial search is unsuccessful. These are starting points, not a required number of searches.

Look beyond a document’s title. Useful information may be spread across a syllabus, a separate objectives page, a weekly schedule, or a departmental outline. Read the available material itself rather than relying on search-result snippets.

For PDFs, inspect relevant pages directly, especially schedules, tables, or scanned content that text extraction may miss. Do not describe a document as fully reviewed when only its preview or an excerpt was accessible.

Use archived official materials when a closer match is unavailable. A third-party copy can supplement the search, but identify it as a reposted document rather than an authenticated university original. Do not assume that its upload date is the course’s teaching term.

If a syllabus is restricted to a learning-management system, record the access limitation. Note the relevant department or instructor contact for obtaining it when needed; do not claim to have reviewed inaccessible content.

## 4. Check Each Source’s Relevance

Evaluate sources by both their authority and their fit to the course being mapped. An official page can still describe the wrong term, campus, or course variant.

For each useful source, record:

- Its title, URL, publisher or host, and document type.
- The course code, term, campus, section, and instructor when available.
- The catalog year or document date, the date accessed, and whether access was complete or partial.
- Any mismatch with the target course, such as an older offering, honors section, or undated sample syllabus.

Use the relevant catalog for the official description and formal prerequisites. Use a matching syllabus or departmental outcomes document for intended learning expectations, and a matching schedule for documented coverage and progression.

Do not treat an undated template as a syllabus for a specific section. Do not silently merge honors-only material, optional enrichment, or instructor-specific additions into a general course map.

When sources differ, record the difference and identify which applies to the intended offering. Do not combine every topic mentioned across several years into one enlarged course. If the difference cannot be resolved, keep it visible as a limitation.

## 5. Extract Information Needed for the Map

Gather information under the following areas. Preserve the source’s terminology, qualifications, and intended level of achievement. Attach a source identifier and, where useful, a page number or section heading to each excerpt or group of notes.

### Course Purpose and Scope

Capture what the course studies, its educational purpose, and the boundaries of its coverage. Include its intended audience or role in a program only when the materials establish those details.

This information will support the Course Description.

### Stated Learning Outcomes

Collect course-level learning outcomes, objectives, or goals. Preserve their meaning and level: an expectation to recognize or explain something is not automatically an expectation to prove it, design with it, or master an advanced application.

Keep course-specific outcomes distinguishable from broader program or general-education outcomes. Retain relevant broader expectations without presenting them as uniquely defining the course.

Do not invent an outcome list when none is published. Record that explicit outcomes were not located and preserve the available coverage notes for later, clearly identified synthesis. A topic heading alone does not establish a particular level of proficiency.

This information will support the Course Outcomes.

### Topics, Methods, and Progression

Capture the major areas of study and the methods or perspectives emphasized. Distinguish required coverage from optional, supplemental, or section-specific material when the source makes that distinction.

Record the sequence or relationships among areas when a schedule or narrative explicitly establishes them. Do not impose a conventional teaching order merely because it seems logical. A provisional schedule should remain identified as provisional.

Use textbook chapter lists only to establish coverage when the course materials assign or identify those chapters. The presence of a textbook title does not establish that the whole book is taught.

This information will support the Course Overview and help interpret the scope of the outcomes.

### Background and Applications

Record formal prerequisites separately from recommended background. Capture explicitly identified applications and connections to further study without adding examples merely because they are common in the subject or relevant to the student’s major.

Include instructional methods or assessment details only when they clarify a substantive learning expectation. Administrative policies, office hours, grading percentages, and assignment deadlines do not ordinarily need to appear in the research brief.

## 6. Prepare a Research Brief for Each Course

Organize the findings so the course map can be written without repeating the research. Keep information traceable to its source rather than producing an unannotated summary from memory.

The following structure can be used for each brief. Replace the placeholders, add entries as needed, and mark missing information explicitly. The example does not prescribe a number of sources, topics, outcomes, or paragraphs.

```markdown
# [University] — [Course Code]: [Official Course Title]

## Course Identity

[Credits, target term or general-course scope, campus or delivery mode,
and confirmed former numbers or variants where relevant.]

## Official Course Description

[Quoted description or clearly labeled paraphrase, with source identifier.]

## Stated Learning Outcomes

[Published outcomes or goals with source identifiers, or a note that
explicit outcomes were not located.]

## Documented Scope and Coverage

[Major areas, methods, emphasis, and required or optional distinctions,
with source identifiers.]

## Documented Progression

[Established sequence or relationships, with source identifiers.
State when the available materials do not establish a sequence.]

## Prerequisites and Applications

[Supported requirements, recommended background, and applications,
with source identifiers.]

## Sources

[Source identifier, document title, verified URL, university or host,
term or date, relevant pages, access date, and applicability notes.]

## Gaps and Limitations

[Missing materials, inaccessible content, source disagreements,
older or variant-section evidence, and unresolved questions.]
```

A brief may contain topic lists or schedule notes even though the finished course map must not contain a separate Course Content section or a numbered topic outline. The research record and the final map serve different purposes.

Keep URLs, source identifiers, and limitation notes in the research brief even when the finished course map will omit references and links. Removing citations from the presentation should not remove the underlying record of support.

## 7. Review and Hand Off to Course-Map Creation

Before writing the map, confirm that the gathered information identifies the correct course and establishes its purpose, scope, and intended level. Check that every substantive note has a source and that missing or conflicting information is visible.

There is no required number of documents. Several partial sources may establish the needed information, while one detailed, matching syllabus may provide much of it. Stop when further searching is unlikely to resolve a meaningful gap or change the map’s scope.

If only a broad description is available, do not present the research as sufficient for a detailed map with fully supported outcomes. Identify which parts can be drafted and what additional evidence is needed. Other gaps may allow a narrower map, provided the missing detail is not silently invented.

Pass the research brief and accessible source materials to the second stage. Use `Guide_to_Creating_a_Course_Map.md` and the course-map template to turn the findings into polished prose and course-level outcomes.

During that stage, keep any synthesized outcomes distinguishable from published institutional outcomes in the working record. The final wording must remain within the scope and level supported by the collected materials.

**The first stage establishes the evidence. The second stage organizes and expresses it as a course map.**
