### tutorial 17592186121035 — Introduction

Given two vectors $\mathbf{a}_1$ and $\mathbf{a}_2,$ the set of all possible linear combinations of $\mathbf{a}_1$ and $\mathbf{a}_2$ is

$$
U = \left\{ x_1 \mathbf{a}_1 + x_2 \mathbf{a}_2 \: : \: x_1,x_2\in\mathbb{R} \right\}.
$$

The set $U$ is also called the **linear span** of $\mathbf{a}_1$ and $\mathbf{a}_2,$ and is denoted by $\textrm{Span}\{ \mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2 \}.$ We say that $\mathbf{a}_1$ and $\mathbf{a}_2$ are the **generators** of $\textrm{Span}\{ \mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2 \}.$

For example, let's find the linear span generated by the following vectors:
$$
\mathbf{a_1} = {\left[ \begin{array}{c}- 1 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{array} \right]}, \quad
	\mathbf{a_2} = {\left[ \begin{array}{c}3 \\ 2 \\ - 1\end{array} \right]} .
$$

From the definition of linear span, we know that if $\mathbf{b} \in \textrm{Span}\{ \mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2 \},$ then $\mathbf{b}$ must take the form

$$
\mathbf{b} = x_1\mathbf{a_1} + x_2\mathbf{a_2}
$$

 for some $x_1,x_2\in\mathbb{R}.$ So, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}\mathbf{b} & ={x}_{1}{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{1}}+{x}_{2}{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{2}} \\  & ={x}_{1}\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}+{x}_{2}\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 2 \\ -1\end{bmatrix} \\  & =\begin{bmatrix}-{x}_{1} \\ {x}_{1} \\ 0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}3{x}_{2} \\ 2{x}_{2} \\ -{x}_{2}\end{bmatrix} \\  & =\begin{bmatrix}-{x}_{1}+3{x}_{2} \\ {x}_{1}+2{x}_{2} \\ -{x}_{2}\end{bmatrix}.\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore the linear span of $\mathbf{a}_1$ and $\mathbf{a}_2$ is
$$
U = \left\{ {\left[ \begin{array}{c}- {x}_{1} + 3 {x}_{2} \\ {x}_{1} + 2 {x}_{2} \\ - {x}_{2}\end{array} \right]} \: : \: x_1,x_2\in\mathbb{R} \right\}.
$$

Geometrically, $\textrm{Span}\{ \mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2 \}$ can be viewed as the plane $\Pi$ that passes through the origin and is parallel to the vectors $\mathbf{a}_1$ and $\mathbf{a}_2,$ as shown below.

### example 17592186119619 — Determining Whether a Vector Lies in the Span of Given Vectors

Determine if $\mathbf{b}$ lies in $\textrm{Span}\{ \mathbf{a_1}, \mathbf{a_2} \},$ where
$$
\mathbf{a_1}={\left[ \begin{array}{c}1 \\ 1 \\ 2\end{array} \right]}, \qquad
	\mathbf{a_2}={\left[ \begin{array}{c}5 \\ 3 \\ 2\end{array} \right]}, \qquad
	\mathbf{b}={\left[ \begin{array}{c}8 \\ 2 \\ - 5\end{array} \right]}.
$$

We know that $\mathbf{b} \in \textrm{Span}\{ \mathbf{a_1}, \mathbf{a_2} \}$ if there exist $x_1$ and $x_2$ such that 

$$
\begin{aligned}{x}_{1}{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{1}}+{x}_{2}{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{2}} & =\mathbf{b} \\ {x}_{1}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}+{x}_{2}\begin{bmatrix}5 \\ 3 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} & =\begin{bmatrix}8 \\ 2 \\ -5\end{bmatrix}.\end{aligned}
$$

So, we have the following system of equations:

$$
\left\{\begin{aligned}{x}_{1}+5{x}_{2} &=8 \\ {x}_{1}+3{x}_{2} &=2 \\ 2{x}_{1}+2{x}_{2} &=-5\end{aligned}\right.
$$

To solve the system above, we set up and row reduce an augmented matrix:

$$
\begin{aligned} & \left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & 5 & 8 \\ 1 & 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 & -5\end{array}\right] & {R}_{2} & :={R}_{2}-{R}_{1} \\  & ∼\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & 5 & 8 \\ 0 & -2 & -6 \\ 2 & 2 & -5\end{array}\right] & {R}_{3} & :={R}_{3}-2{R}_{1} \\  & ∼\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & 5 & 8 \\ 0 & -2 & -6 \\ 0 & -8 & -21\end{array}\right] & {R}_{2} & :=-\frac{1}{2}{R}_{2} \\  & ∼\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & 5 & 8 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & -8 & -21\end{array}\right] & {R}_{3} & :={R}_{3}+8{R}_{2} \\  & ∼\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & 5 & 8 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 3\end{array}\right] & {R}_{2} & ↔{R}_{3}\end{aligned}
$$

The third row of the augmented matrix now corresponds to the equation $0x_1 + 0x_2 = 3,$ which has no solution.

So, there are no values of $x_1$ and $x_2$ such that 

$$
\begin{aligned}{x}_{1}{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{1}}+{x}_{2}{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{2}} & =\mathbf{b} \\ {x}_{1}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}+{x}_{2}\begin{bmatrix}5 \\ 2 \\ 3\end{bmatrix} & =\begin{bmatrix}8 \\ -5 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}.\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we conclude that $\mathbf{b}$ does *not* lie in $\textrm{Span}\{ \mathbf{a_1}, \mathbf{a_2} \}.$

### example 17592186119671 — Finding the Value of an Unknown Such That a Vector Lies in the Span of Given Vectors

Find the values of $k$ such that the vector $\mathbf{b}$ lies in $\textrm{Span}\{ \mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2 \},$ where
$$
\mathbf{a_1}={\left[ \begin{array}{c}1 \\ 5 \\ 7\end{array} \right]}, \qquad
	\mathbf{a_2}={\left[ \begin{array}{c}- 2 \\ 4 \\ 2\end{array} \right]}, \qquad
	\mathbf{b}={\left[ \begin{array}{c}13 \\ - 5 \\ k\end{array} \right]}.
$$

We know that $\mathbf{b} \in \textrm{Span}\{ \mathbf{a_1}, \mathbf{a_2} \}$ if there exist $x_1$ and $x_2$ such that 

$$
\begin{aligned}{x}_{1}{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{1}}+{x}_{2}{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{2}} & =\mathbf{b} \\ {x}_{1}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 5 \\ 7\end{bmatrix}+{x}_{2}\begin{bmatrix}-2 \\ 4 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} & =\begin{bmatrix}13 \\ -5 \\ k\end{bmatrix}.\end{aligned}
$$

So, we have the following system of equations:

$$
\left\{\begin{aligned}{x}_{1}-2{x}_{2} &=13 \\ 5{x}_{1}+4{x}_{2} &=-5 \\ 7{x}_{1}+2{x}_{2} &=k\end{aligned}\right.
$$

To solve the system above, we set up and row reduce an augmented matrix:

$$
\begin{aligned} & \left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & -2 & 13 \\ 5 & 4 & -5 \\ 7 & 2 & k\end{array}\right] & {R}_{2} & :={R}_{2}-5{R}_{1} \\  & ∼\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & -2 & 13 \\ 0 & 14 & -70 \\ 7 & 2 & k\end{array}\right] & {R}_{3} & :={R}_{3}-7{R}_{1} \\  & ∼\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & -2 & 13 \\ 0 & 14 & -70 \\ 0 & 16 & k-91\end{array}\right] & {R}_{2} & :=\frac{1}{14}{R}_{2} \\  & ∼\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & -2 & 13 \\ 0 & 1 & -5 \\ 0 & 16 & k-91\end{array}\right] & {R}_{3} & :={R}_{3}-16{R}_{2} \\  & ∼\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & -2 & 13 \\ 0 & 1 & -5 \\ 0 & 0 & k-11\end{array}\right]\end{aligned}
$$

The third row of the augmented matrix now corresponds to the equation $0x_1 + 0x_2 = k-11$. Therefore, the system has a solution only if

$$
0 = k-11 \quad \Rightarrow \quad k=11.
$$

### tutorial 17592186121036 — Geometric Interpretation of the Linear Span

The linear span of vectors can be interpreted geometrically as the points in space that can be reached by adding multiples of those vectors.

- The span of a single nonzero vector is a *line* through the origin. More precisely, if $\mathbf{a} \neq \mathbf{0},$ then $\textrm{Span}\{ \mathbf{a} \}$ determines the *line* with direction vector $\mathbf{a}$ that passes through the origin. For instance, if
$$
\mathbf{a} = {\left[ \begin{array}{c}1 \\ - 1 \\ 2\end{array} \right]},
$$
then $\textrm{Span}\{ \mathbf{a} \}$ represents the line $l$ *spanned* by $\mathbf{a}\mathbin{:}$

![](images/08/080aa6203494e915e28c491a3d86176f7ba634bcbdfe40b094c07d85fb29bb69.png)

- The span of two non-parallel vectors is a *plane* through the origin. More precisely, if $\mathbf{a} \not\parallel \mathbf{b}$ ($\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are not parallel), then $\textrm{Span}\{ \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \}$ determines the *plane* with direction vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ that passes through the origin. For instance, if
$$
\mathbf{a} = {\left[ \begin{array}{c}1 \\ - 1 \\ 2\end{array} \right]},
	\qquad
	\mathbf{b} = {\left[ \begin{array}{c}- 1 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{array} \right]},
$$
then $\textrm{Span}\{ \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \}$  represents the plane $\Pi$ *spanned* by $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}\mathbin{:}$

![](images/56/5606535bd91eb35745a9852ceb451720339105f5a97fb66ccfcb1164ecd55ffd.png)



### example 17592186119620 — Determining Whether a Vector Lies on a Plane

Determine if the vector $\mathbf{u}$ lies on the plane $\;\Pi \,:\, \mathbf{r}=s \mathbf{b} + t \mathbf{c}, \, t,s \in \mathbb{R},$ where  $\mathbf{b}=\langle 1, 4, 5 \rangle, \mathbf{c} = \langle 2, 4, 6 \rangle$ and  $\mathbf{u}= \langle 2, 10, 12 \rangle.$

By definition, $\mathbf{u} \in \Pi$ if there exist $t$ and $s$ such that

$$
\begin{aligned}s\mathbf{b}+t\mathbf{c} & =\mathbf{u} \\ s\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 4 \\ 5\end{bmatrix}+t\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 4 \\ 6\end{bmatrix} & =\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 10 \\ 12\end{bmatrix}.\end{aligned}
$$

So, we have the following system of equations:

$$
\left\{\begin{aligned}s+2t &=2 \\ 4s+4t &=10 \\ 5s+6t &=12\end{aligned}\right.
$$

To solve the system above, we set up and row reduce an augmented matrix:

$$
\begin{aligned} & \left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & 2 & 2 \\ 4 & 4 & 10 \\ 5 & 6 & 12\end{array}\right] & {R}_{2} & :={R}_{2}-4{R}_{1} \\  & ∼\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & 2 & 2 \\ 0 & -4 & 2 \\ 5 & 6 & 12\end{array}\right] & {R}_{3} & :={R}_{3}-5{R}_{1} \\  & ∼\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & 2 & 2 \\ 0 & -4 & 2 \\ 0 & -4 & 2\end{array}\right] & {R}_{3} & :={R}_{3}-{R}_{2} \\  & ∼\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & 2 & 2 \\ 0 & -4 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{array}\right]\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we have the system

$$
\left\{\begin{aligned}s+2t &=2 \\ -4t &=2 \\ 0 &=0.\end{aligned}\right.
$$

From the second equation, we get

$$
-4t = 2 \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad t = -\dfrac{1}{2}.
$$

Substituting into the first equation, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}s+2t & =2 \\ s+2\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) & =2 \\ s-1 & =2 \\ s & =3.\end{aligned}
$$

Since we found a solution $s=3, \, t=-\dfrac{1}{2},$ we conclude that $\mathbf{u}$ does indeed lie on the plane $\Pi.$ In fact, we have

$$
\mathbf{u}=3\mathbf{b} - \dfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{c}.
$$

The vectors ${\mathbf{a}}_{1}$, ${\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{2}}$, and $\mathbf{b}$ are given below. Determine if $\mathbf{b}\in \text{Span}\{{\mathbf{a}}_{1},{\mathbf{a}}_{2}\}.$ In the affirmative case, find the value of ${x}_{1}\cdot {x}_{2}$ from the decomposition $\mathbf{b}={x}_{1}{\mathbf{a}}_{1}+{x}_{2}{\mathbf{a}}_{2}.$

$$
{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{1}}=\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -4 \\ -7\end{bmatrix},\hspace{2em}{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{2}}=\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ -3 \\ -5\end{bmatrix},\hspace{2em}\mathbf{b}=\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ -6 \\ -11\end{bmatrix}
$$

Solution: We know that $\mathbf{b}\in \text{Span}\{{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{1}},{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{2}}\}$ if there exist ${x}_{1}$ and ${x}_{2}$ such that 

$$
\begin{aligned}{x}_{1}{\mathbf{a}}_{1}+{x}_{2}{\mathbf{a}}_{2} & =\mathbf{b} \\ {x}_{1}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -4 \\ -7\end{bmatrix}+{x}_{2}\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ -3 \\ -5\end{bmatrix} & =\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ -6 \\ -11\end{bmatrix}.\end{aligned}
$$

So, we have the following system of equations:

$$
\left\{\begin{aligned}{x}_{1}+2{x}_{2} &=-1 \\ -4{x}_{1}-3{x}_{2} &=-6 \\ -7{x}_{1}-5{x}_{2} &=-11\end{aligned}\right.
$$

To solve the system above, we set up and row reduce an augmented matrix:

$$
\begin{aligned} & \left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & 2 & -1 \\ -4 & -3 & -6 \\ -7 & -5 & -11\end{array}\right] & {R}_{2} & :={R}_{2}+4{R}_{1} \\  & ∼\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 5 & -10 \\ -7 & -5 & -11\end{array}\right] & {R}_{3} & :={R}_{3}+7{R}_{1} \\  & ∼\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 5 & -10 \\ 0 & 9 & -18\end{array}\right] & {R}_{2} & :=\frac{1}{5}{R}_{2} \\  & ∼\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 9 & -18\end{array}\right] & {R}_{3} & :={R}_{3}-9{R}_{2} \\  & ∼\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{array}\right]\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we have the system

$$
\left\{\begin{aligned}{x}_{1}+2{x}_{2} &=-1 \\ {x}_{2} &=-2 \\ 0 &=0.\end{aligned}\right.
$$

From the second equation, we get ${x}_{2}=-2.$ Substituting into the first equation gives 

$$
{x}_{1}+2(-2)=-1\,⟹\,{x}_{1}=3.
$$

Since the system has a solution, we can conclude that $\mathbf{b}\in \text{Span}\{{\mathbf{a}}_{1},{\mathbf{a}}_{2}\}.$

Moreover, there is exactly one solution, so we have exactly one way to write $\mathbf{b}$ as a linear combination of ${\mathbf{a}}_{1}$ and ${\mathbf{a}}_{2}:$

$$
\begin{aligned}3{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{1}}+(-2){\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{2}} & =\mathbf{b} \\ 3\cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -4 \\ -7\end{bmatrix}+(-2)\cdot \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ -3 \\ -5\end{bmatrix} & =\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ -6 \\ -11\end{bmatrix}\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, ${x}_{1}\cdot {x}_{2}=3\cdot (-2)=-6.$
