Determine whether

det(A+B)=det(A)+det(B).

If it is true, prove it. Otherwise, give a counterexample. If it is false in general, for what size matrices is the statement true?

Here A and B are square matrices of the same size.

Your original response — Jacob Peterson (entry 43183864)

The statement is false for matrices larger than 1x1. Given:

A=B=[1001]

Adding the matrices and calculating the determinants gives:

det(A+B)=|2002|=4

det(A)+det(B)=1+1=2

Since 4 does not equal 2, the statement is false.

However, it is true for 1x1 matrices because the determinant is just the single entry.

A=[a],B=[b]

det(A+B)=a+b=det(A)+det(B)

Therefore, the statement is always true for 1x1 matrices, but not for matrices 2x2 or larger.

Authored verification. Your 2×2 counterexample is correct: A=B=I2 gives A+B=2I2, whose determinant is 2⋅2−0⋅0=4, while det(A)+det(B)=1+1=2. Your argument for 1×1 matrices also proves the identity for every pair of that size.

To justify the conclusion for every larger size, take A=B=In. Then

det(A+B)=det(2In)=2n,det(A)+det(B)=2.

For every integer n≥2, 2n>2. Thus, among positive matrix sizes, 1×1 is the only size for which the equality holds for all pairs. It can still hold for particular larger pairs, for example when B=0; the conclusion concerns a universal identity.

The determinant is linear in one row at a time when all other rows are fixed. That property does not imply additivity when an entire matrix is added.
