Given that ${y}_{1}(x)=x{e}^{-3x}$ is a solution to the equation ${y}^{″}+6{y}^{′}+9y=0,$ then according to the superposition principle which of the following must also be a solution to the equation?

I. $y(x)={x}^{2}{e}^{-3x}$

II. $y(x)=x{e}^{1-3x}$

III. $y(x)=\frac{x{e}^{-3x}}{2}$

=== 17592186445225 :question/worked-solution ===
According to the superposition principle, if the function ${y}_{1}(x)$ is a solution to a homogeneous, linear differential equation, and $c$ is a constant, then $y(x)=c{y}_{1}(x)$ is also a solution to the equation.

Let's consider each option:

- The function $y(x)={x}^{2}{e}^{-3x}=x\cdot x{e}^{-3x}$ is **not** guaranteed to be a solution of the equation by the superposition principle. The superposition principle does not extend to the product of a non-constant function and a known solution.

- The function $y(x)=x{e}^{1-3x}=e\cdot x{e}^{-3x}$ is a solution of the equation by the superposition principle. In this case, $c=e.$

- The function $y(x)=\frac{x{e}^{-3x}}{2}$ is a solution of the equation by the superposition principle. In this case, $c=\frac{1}{2}.$

Therefore, the correct answer is "II and III only."

Recall that a second-order linear ODE can always be expressed in the form

$$
a(x)y'' + b(x)y' + c(x) y = f(x).
$$

Also, recall that the equation is *homogeneous* if $f(x) = 0$ for all $x{:}$

$$
a(x)y'' + b(x)y' + c(x) y =0
$$

The **superposition principle** states that if the functions $y_1(x)$ and $y_2(x)$ are solutions to a linear homogeneous differential equation, then the function 

$$
y(x) = y_1(x) + y_2(x)
$$

is also a solution to the equation.

To illustrate, consider the following homogeneous differential equation:

$$
y'' + 16y = 0
$$

 

Two solutions of this equation are

$$
y_1(x) = \cos 4x, \qquad y_2(x) = \sin 4x.
$$

We can easily check that these are solutions by substituting them into the equation and verifying that they result in true statements:

$$
\begin{aligned}{y}_{1}^{''}+16{y}_{1} & =0 \\ (\operatorname{cos}4x)^{''}+16\operatorname{cos}4x & =0 \\ (-4\operatorname{sin}4x)^{'}+16\operatorname{cos}4x & =0 \\ -16\operatorname{cos}4x+16\operatorname{cos}4x & =0 \\ 0 & =0\,✓ \\ {y}_{2}^{''}+16{y}_{2} & =0 \\ (\operatorname{sin}4x)^{''}+16\operatorname{sin}4x & =0 \\ (4\operatorname{cos}4x)^{'}+16\operatorname{sin}4x & =0 \\ -16\operatorname{sin}4x+16\operatorname{sin}4x & =0 \\ 0 & =0\,✓\end{aligned}
$$

Because the equation is linear and homogeneous, the superposition principle guarantees that the sum

$$
\begin{aligned}y & ={y}_{1}+{y}_{2}=\operatorname{cos}4x+\operatorname{sin}4x\end{aligned}
$$

is also a solution to the differential equation. 

We can check that this is a solution to the equation by substituting (keeping the order consistent with our definition):

$$
\begin{aligned}{y}^{''}+16y & =0 \\ (\operatorname{cos}4x+\operatorname{sin}4x)^{''}+16(\operatorname{cos}4x+\operatorname{sin}4x) & =0 \\ (-4\operatorname{sin}4x+4\operatorname{cos}4x)^{'}+16(\operatorname{cos}4x+\operatorname{sin}4x) & =0 \\ (-16\operatorname{cos}4x-16\operatorname{sin}4x)+16\operatorname{cos}4x+16\operatorname{sin}4x & =0 \\ 0=0\,✓\end{aligned}
$$

**Watch out!** The superposition principle does *not* extend to the products or quotients of the known solutions. For example, the functions

$$
\cos4x\sin4x, \quad \sin^24x, \quad \dfrac{\sin4x}{\cos 4x},
$$

are *not* solutions to our original differential equation.

Suppose we have the second-order homogeneous ODE

$$
a(x)y'' + b(x)y' + c(x) y = 0.
$$

The superposition principle also states that if $y_1(x)$ is a solution to the equation, then the function 

$$
y(x) = cy_1(x)
$$

is also a solution to the equation, where $c$ is a constant. 

To illustrate, let's once again consider the following linear differential equation:

$$
y'' + 16y = 0
$$

 

Recall that

$$
y_1(x) = \cos 4x, \qquad y_2(x) = \sin 4x
$$

 are solutions to this equation. So, according to the superposition principle, the functions

$$
6\cos 4x, \quad -\sin 4x, \qquad \dfrac{\pi}{e}\cos 4x
$$

are all solutions to the ODE.

We can check that these are indeed solutions to the equation by substituting. To illustrate, let's verify the solution $6\cos 4x = 6y_1(x)$ by evaluating the left-hand side of this equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}{y}^{''}+16y & =(6\operatorname{cos}4x)^{''}+16(6\operatorname{cos}4x) \\  & =6\cdot (\operatorname{cos}4x)^{''}+6\cdot 16(\operatorname{cos}4x) \\  & =6\cdot ((\operatorname{cos}4x)^{''}+16\operatorname{cos}4x) \\  & =6\cdot ((-4\operatorname{sin}4x)^{'}+16\operatorname{cos}4x) \\  & =6\cdot (-16\operatorname{cos}4x+16\operatorname{cos}4x) \\  & =6\cdot 0 \\  & =0\,✓\end{aligned}
$$

**Watch out!** This idea extends to *scalar* (or constant) multiples only. For example, the function $x\cos 4x$ is *not* a solution to our ODE.

=== 17592186153384 :tutorial/content ===
In its most general form, the **superposition principle** states that any linear combination (or "superposition") of solutions to a homogeneous, linear differential equation is also a solution. More precisely:

> *If the functions $y_1(x)$ and $y_2(x)$ are solutions to a homogeneous, linear differential equation, and $c_1$ and $c_2$ are constants, then $y(x) = c_1 y_1(x) + c_2 y_2(x)$ is also a solution to the equation.*

Note that the superposition principle follows immediately from the definition of a linear differential operator $L.$ Recall that every homogeneous equation can be written as

$$
L(y) = 0.
$$

Now, let $y = c_1 y_1(x) + c_2y_2(x).$ Then, by the linearity of $L,$ we have

$$
\begin{aligned}L(y) & =L({c}_{1}{y}_{1}(x)+{c}_{2}{y}_{2}(x)) \\  & =L({c}_{1}{y}_{1}(x))+L({c}_{2}{y}_{2}(x)) \\  & ={c}_{1}L({y}_{1}(x))+{c}_{2}L({y}_{2}(x)) \\  & ={c}_{1}\cdot 0+{c}_{2}\cdot 0 \\  & =0+0 \\  & =0.\end{aligned}
$$

For example, suppose we're told that the differential equation

$$
y'' - y = 0
$$

has two solutions

$$
y_1(x) = e^x \quad \textrm{and} \quad y_2(x) = e^{-x}.
$$

Then, according to the superposition principle, any linear combination

$$
y(x) = c_1 e^x + c_2 e^{-x}
$$

is also a solution.

- For instance, we can conclude that

$$
\begin{aligned}y(x) & =2{y}_{1}(x)-\frac{1}{3}{y}_{2}(x) \\  & =2{e}^{x}-\frac{1}{3}{e}^{-x}\end{aligned}
$$

is also a solution because it takes the form $c_1 e^x + c_2 e^{-x}$ with $c_1 = 2$ and $c_2 = -\dfrac{1}{3}.$ $\quad \color{green}\checkmark$

- However, we *cannot* conclude that

$$
\begin{aligned}y(x) & ={x}^{2}{y}_{1}(x)+\operatorname{sin}(x){y}_{2}(x) \\  & ={x}^{2}{e}^{x}+\operatorname{sin}(x){e}^{-x}\end{aligned}
$$

is a solution because $x^2$ and $\sin(x)$ are not constants. $\quad \color{red}\times$
