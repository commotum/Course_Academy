# Scaffolding and Adaptive Difficulty in *The Math Academy Way*

## Main finding

*The Math Academy Way* strongly supports finely scaffolded instruction, progressively more demanding practice, and adaptation to a learner’s **demonstrated proficiency on particular skills**. Its central recommendation is to keep tasks challenging enough to improve performance while sufficiently supported and appropriately scoped that the learner can succeed.

The strongest sources are **Chapter 14, “Minimizing Cognitive Load,” Chapter 12, “Deliberate Practice,” Chapter 13, “Mastery Learning,”** and **Chapter 20, “The Testing Effect (Retrieval Practice).”** The FAQs give particularly direct descriptions of progression through knowledge points and adaptation based on performance.

These passages provide a strong rationale for a question bank with **easy, moderate, and hard variants**: it supplies accessible entry tasks, opportunities to consolidate a skill, and opportunities to extend and assess proficiency. However, the review did **not** locate an explicit requirement for exactly three difficulty tiers inside every knowledge point. The book directly describes increasing difficulty **across knowledge points**, worked examples and similar practice **within knowledge points**, and increasingly demanding conditions during subsequent reviews and quizzes. A three-tier bank within each knowledge point is an implementation recommendation derived from these principles.

## Scope and evidence boundaries

The review searched the local chapters, FAQs, and back matter for scaffolding, cognitive load, difficulty, challenge, proficiency, knowledge points, mastery, and related language. Relevant passages were read in context, including passages cautioning against excessive scaffolding or difficulty introduced too early.

This report distinguishes the book’s explicit recommendations, research findings as presented by the book, and implications for designing question banks. It does not independently evaluate the cited research papers. Numerical claims below retain their original context; they should not be treated as universal thresholds for every learning activity.

## 1. Scaffolding prevents cognitive overload

### Chapter 14 — The Learning Staircase

The chapter compares learning to climbing stairs: a step can be too large for a particular learner. Breaking it into smaller steps makes the progression accessible. In cognitive terms, a task becomes unmanageable when its working-memory demands exceed the learner’s capacity.

The book describes its topic structure as several knowledge points or stages of increasing difficulty. It also explains that complex, multipart problems can be scaffolded one part at a time, with each part using a previously learned skill.

**Implication:** A learner needs an accessible route into a topic before being asked to execute its more demanding cases. If the bank contains only advanced questions, instruction can stall before the learner develops the component skills needed to solve them.

[Source: Chapter 14, “The Learning Staircase”](../The%20Math%20Academy%20Way/III-COGNITIVE-LEARNING-STRATEGIES/14-Minimizing-Cognitive-Load/14-Minimizing-Cognitive-Load.md#the-learning-staircase)

### Chapter 14 — Micro-Scaffolding

The chapter explicitly addresses support **within each knowledge point**:

> “Each knowledge point starts with a demonstration or worked example”

Students then solve similar problems and progress to the next knowledge point only after demonstrating mastery. The discussion cites Sweller (2006) for the worked-example effect. It also describes subgoal labeling—grouping solution steps into meaningful units—to reduce working-memory demands and support transfer to novel problems in the same category, citing Catrambone (1995).

**Implication:** Accessible questions should practice the demonstrated move. When a question introduces another substantial logical leap or component skill, a new worked example or intermediate knowledge point may be needed. Merely labeling that question “hard” does not provide the missing instruction.

[Source: Chapter 14, “Micro-Scaffolding”](../The%20Math%20Academy%20Way/III-COGNITIVE-LEARNING-STRATEGIES/14-Minimizing-Cognitive-Load/14-Minimizing-Cognitive-Load.md#micro-scaffolding)

## 2. The ideal challenge is achievable, with enough support

### Chapter 12 — Discomfort is Required

This section provides the clearest statement of the desired balance:

> “maximum-efficiency learning should feel just-enough scaffolded that the learning tasks are challenging yet still achievable.”

It then describes maximizing task difficulty subject to the learner successfully overcoming the difficulty in a timely manner. The surrounding discussion distinguishes deliberate practice at the edge of current ability from repeatedly performing familiar tasks within one’s comfort zone.

The chapter quotes Lehtinen et al. (2017), describing geometry research by Pachman, Sweller, and Kalyuga (2013): more knowledgeable students tended to select achievable rather than difficult problems when given a choice, with minimal performance improvements; their skills improved when a deliberate-practice model assigned difficult problems. This is a finding as presented by the book, rather than an independent assessment of that study.

**Implication:** A bank needs questions that stretch learners who have already mastered its introductory cases. At the same time, “hard” should represent a reachable extension for a prepared learner, rather than an arbitrary obstacle or a prolonged research problem.

[Source: Chapter 12, “Discomfort is Required”](../The%20Math%20Academy%20Way/III-COGNITIVE-LEARNING-STRATEGIES/12-Deliberate-Practice/12-Deliberate-Practice.md#discomfort-is-required)

### Chapter 20 — Desirable vs. Undesirable Difficulties

The book states:

> “Introducing an insurmountable difficulty is never desirable”

It explains that a difficulty may become beneficial later, once the learner has sufficient proficiency. It quotes Brown, Roediger, and McDaniel (2014) and Bjork and Bjork (2023) on the need for sufficient background knowledge and skills to respond successfully. The optimal difficulty can vary considerably with a student’s prior knowledge.

The section also reports Codding, VanDerHeyden, and Chehayeb (2023) as finding that instructional strategies mismatched to the student’s stage of skill development can fail to improve performance and may increase anxiety—even when the target skill itself is appropriate.

**Implication:** Difficulty must be calibrated to the learner’s current proficiency and the activity’s purpose. Assigning the same demanding problem to everyone can produce productive practice for one student and an instructional mismatch for another.

[Source: Chapter 20, “Desirable vs. Undesirable Difficulties”](../The%20Math%20Academy%20Way/III-COGNITIVE-LEARNING-STRATEGIES/20-The-Testing-Effect-Retrieval-Practice/20-The-Testing-Effect-Retrieval-Practice.md#desirable-vs-undesirable-difficulties)

## 3. Progression should follow demonstrated mastery

### Chapter 13 — Implementing Mastery Learning

The chapter contrasts advancing through a fixed schedule with selecting topics whose prerequisites the student has demonstrated sufficient proficiency on. If the student gets stuck, they can work on other topics that do not depend on the problematic one before trying again.

**Implication:** Advancement should depend on evidence of readiness. A learner may be ready for demanding questions in one area while needing introductory practice or prerequisite repair in another.

[Source: Chapter 13, “Implementing Mastery Learning”](../The%20Math%20Academy%20Way/III-COGNITIVE-LEARNING-STRATEGIES/13-Mastery-Learning/13-Mastery-Learning.md#implementing-mastery-learning)

### Chapter 13 — Knowledge Frontier as Zone of Proximal Development

The book connects the knowledge frontier to tasks the learner can complete with support but cannot yet complete independently. It recommends selecting new topics for which the learner has mastered the prerequisites.

The chapter cites Zou et al. (2019), reporting that students on another platform were approximately **3–4 times as likely to master a topic when it lay along their knowledge frontier** as when it lay beyond it. This finding concerns topic selection on that platform; it is not a measured effect of three-tier question banks.

**Implication:** Appropriate challenge requires information about prerequisite readiness. Easier variants can supply an entry point, but repeated difficulty caused by a missing prerequisite may require targeted practice on that prerequisite.

[Source: Chapter 13, “Knowledge Frontier as Zone of Proximal Development”](../The%20Math%20Academy%20Way/III-COGNITIVE-LEARNING-STRATEGIES/13-Mastery-Learning/13-Mastery-Learning.md#knowledge-frontier-as-zone-of-proximal-development)

### Chapter 4 — Scaffolded Mastery Learning

This section directly describes the progression from the most basic idea or skill in the first knowledge point to more advanced cases in later knowledge points. Topic mastery requires sufficiently many correct answers in each successive knowledge point. The following section links each knowledge point to key prerequisites for targeted remediation.

**Implication:** Topic-level coverage must include the more advanced cases, while progression should preserve visibility into which individual knowledge point the learner can perform.

[Source: Chapter 4, “Scaffolded Mastery Learning”](../The%20Math%20Academy%20Way/I-PRELIMINARIES/4-Core-Technology-the-Knowledge-Graph/4-Core-Technology-the-Knowledge-Graph.md#scaffolded-mastery-learning)

## 4. Support should change as proficiency improves

### Chapter 14 — The Expertise Reversal Effect

The chapter says scaffolding should be gradually removed as learners become comfortable with the material. Lessons present similar questions beside worked examples; reviews mix question types so the appropriate reference is less obvious; quizzes remove access to the lessons and add timing.

**Implication:** A question’s difficulty depends on both its content and its conditions. The same question can place different demands on a learner when it appears next to a worked example, in a mixed review, or on a timed assessment without reference material.

[Source: Chapter 14, “The Expertise Reversal Effect”](../The%20Math%20Academy%20Way/III-COGNITIVE-LEARNING-STRATEGIES/14-Minimizing-Cognitive-Load/14-Minimizing-Cognitive-Load.md#the-expertise-reversal-effect)

### Chapter 20 — Appropriate vs. Inappropriate Usage of Timed Tests; Implementing Appropriate Timed Testing

The chapter states that learners should be able to perform a skill successfully in an untimed setting before timed testing. It describes adapting instruction to each individual student on each individual skill, with highly scaffolded initial lessons followed by higher-intensity practice after demonstrated proficiency.

**Implication:** Difficulty progression includes a transition from acquiring a skill to performing it reliably, retrieving it independently, and eventually executing it fluently. Speed should not be used as the initial challenge when the learner is still acquiring the method.

[Source: Chapter 20, “Appropriate vs. Inappropriate Usage of Timed Tests”](../The%20Math%20Academy%20Way/III-COGNITIVE-LEARNING-STRATEGIES/20-The-Testing-Effect-Retrieval-Practice/20-The-Testing-Effect-Retrieval-Practice.md#appropriate-vs-inappropriate-usage-of-timed-tests)

[Source: Chapter 20, “Implementing Appropriate Timed Testing”](../The%20Math%20Academy%20Way/III-COGNITIVE-LEARNING-STRATEGIES/20-The-Testing-Effect-Retrieval-Practice/20-The-Testing-Effect-Retrieval-Practice.md#implementing-appropriate-timed-testing)

## 5. The FAQs explicitly describe adaptive practice

### FAQ: The Practice Experience — Active Learning, Scaffolding, and Automaticity

Under **“How does a lesson work?”**, the FAQ describes a worked example followed by 2–5 practice questions, with the number adapting to performance:

> “practice questions continue until the student demonstrates sufficient mastery of the KP to continue building more advanced learning on top of that understanding.”

A typical lesson has three or four knowledge points of increasing difficulty, starting with the simplest case and extending to more advanced cases. The same section distinguishes **baseline mastery**—consistent successful problem-solving sufficient to build on—from automaticity, which develops over later practice.

Under **“I perform well in the lessons themselves, but do not recall or recognize patterns as well when doing quizzes. Is this normal?”**, the FAQ says quiz difficulty increases when the learner is doing well and calls **80–85% accuracy** the learning sweet spot. That is the FAQ’s statement about quizzes. It should not be converted into a universal mastery threshold or a requirement that introductory practice have a 15–20% error rate. The reported lesson pass rates of 95% on the first attempt and 99% within two attempts concern a different measure and activity.

**Implication:** Practice volume, challenge, and progression should respond to performance. Successful scaffolded practice establishes readiness for later demands, while independent assessment checks what the learner can do without the initial supports.

[Source: FAQ: The Practice Experience, “Active Learning, Scaffolding, and Automaticity”](../The%20Math%20Academy%20Way/VI-FREQUENTLY-ASKED-QUESTIONS/FAQ-The-Practice-Experience/FAQ-The-Practice-Experience.md#active-learning-scaffolding-and-automaticity)

### FAQ: Miscellaneous — Features that Do Not Exist for a Good Reason

Under **“Why can’t I edit my knowledge profile?”**, the book explains that self-reported knowledge can be overestimated:

> “To construct an accurate knowledge profile, the system must infer it from a student's demonstrated ability to solve problems.”

Later, in the answer about conversational AI, it says learners should begin with simple cases and climb the ladder of difficulty, covering the cases they could reasonably be expected to solve on a future assessment. It also recommends personalized remediation based on incorrect answers and advancement after demonstrated prerequisite mastery.

**Implication:** Difficulty selection should use actual problem-solving performance. A learner’s confidence or claim to understand an explanation is insufficient evidence of readiness for advanced cases.

[Source: FAQ: Miscellaneous, “Features that Do Not Exist for a Good Reason”](../The%20Math%20Academy%20Way/VI-FREQUENTLY-ASKED-QUESTIONS/FAQ-Miscellaneous/FAQ-Miscellaneous.md#features-that-do-not-exist-for-a-good-reason)

## 6. Difficulty ranges must preserve coverage and standards

### Chapter 16 — How We Layer

The chapter states that a lesson should cover all problem types a student could reasonably be expected to solve if they truly know the topic. It criticizes courses that cover only the simplest cases, because learners then cannot solve standard problems outside that narrow set and miss opportunities to reinforce prerequisite skills.

**Implication:** An easy entry point is valuable, but a topic bank also needs its substantive extensions. The advanced end of the bank should represent genuine topic coverage.

[Source: Chapter 16, “How We Layer”](../The%20Math%20Academy%20Way/III-COGNITIVE-LEARNING-STRATEGIES/16-Layering/16-Layering.md#how-we-layer)

### Chapter 30 — Conservative vs. Aggressive Edge of Mastery; Selecting Good Diagnostic Questions

The chapter says a placement exam must cover the most advanced question type from each topic, because solving only simpler cases cannot justify placing the learner out of that topic. It also distinguishes lesson-level readiness to build on a skill from stronger performance developed through subsequent practice.

For selecting individual diagnostic questions, it adds a useful qualification: a diagnostic item should be difficult enough to establish topic mastery and exercise all prerequisites, but generally should not be the topic’s most difficult question. Its preferred item is the simplest one meeting those evidentiary requirements.

**Implication:** A bank should include representative advanced cases, but assessment should select informative questions rather than maximize difficulty for its own sake. The two sections distinguish coverage of advanced question types from choosing an unnecessarily complicated individual item.

[Source: Chapter 30, “Conservative vs. Aggressive Edge of Mastery”](../The%20Math%20Academy%20Way/V-TECHNICAL-DEEP-DIVES/30-Technical-Deep-Dive-on-Diagnostic-Exams/30-Technical-Deep-Dive-on-Diagnostic-Exams.md#conservative-vs-aggressive-edge-of-mastery)

## Implications for easy, moderate, and hard question banks

The following is a design interpretation of the evidence above, rather than a tier system explicitly prescribed by the book.

| Tier | Intended role | Design requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Easy | Establish an accessible entry point and initial execution of the target move. | Use a simple representative case aligned with the worked example; keep incidental demands low. |
| Moderate | Consolidate reliable execution and meaningful variation. | Vary the cases the learner is expected to handle while preserving the knowledge point’s taught skill and prerequisites. |
| Hard | Extend proficiency and check command of demanding, representative cases. | Increase relevant demands or reduce support after demonstrated readiness; teach any substantial new move before assessing it. |

A bank covering this range gives instruction room to adapt. Introductory items can reveal whether a learner can execute the basic method; intermediate items can test reliability across variations; advanced items can expose incomplete coverage or readiness for further progression. The difficulty of an item remains relative to the learner’s current knowledge and the conditions under which it is attempted.

At the **topic level**, the book directly supports progression through increasingly advanced cases and coverage beyond the simplest examples. At the **knowledge-point level**, it directly supports worked examples, similar practice, demonstrated mastery, and performance analysis at the level of individual questions. Offering three meaningful tiers within a knowledge point is a reasonable way to provide adaptive options where the skill permits them. A variant requiring a substantial new skill may belong in another knowledge point instead.

For using such a bank, the evidence supports these principles:

1. **Start at an appropriate entry point.** Use evidence of prerequisite readiness and current performance to select the initial challenge and support.
2. **Advance after consistent successful execution.** Do not infer topic mastery solely from introductory items or one correct response. The practice FAQ explicitly treats consistency and the sequence of responses as informative.
3. **Respond to struggle precisely.** Additional practice, a smaller instructional step, or repair of a missing prerequisite may be appropriate. Persistent difficulty should also prompt inspection of the question or explanation.
4. **Gradually increase independence.** Fade worked-example support and introduce mixed retrieval or timing at the appropriate stage.
5. **Retain meaningful advanced coverage.** Accessible instruction should prepare learners for the full expected scope of the topic.
6. **Check learning beyond the scaffolded setting.** Review and independent assessment determine whether success transfers after the immediate cues are removed.

The review does not establish that every learner must answer every tier, that tiers should appear in equal proportions, or that exactly three tiers is optimal for every knowledge point. Those are implementation choices to evaluate through learner performance.

## Evidence-backed rationale for reuse

> Question banks should provide a meaningful range of difficulty so instruction can match the learner’s demonstrated proficiency. Accessible questions and worked examples establish a usable foundation; progressively more demanding questions extend performance and cover the topic’s advanced cases. Advancement should follow demonstrated mastery, and support should be adjusted when the learner struggles or becomes more proficient. *The Math Academy Way* supports these principles through its discussions of cognitive load, deliberate practice, mastery learning, expertise reversal, and targeted remediation. Organizing the range into easy, moderate, and hard tiers is a practical implementation of those principles, rather than a three-tier rule stated by the book.
