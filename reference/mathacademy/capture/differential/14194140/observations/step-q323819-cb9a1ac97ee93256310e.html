Given a polynomial with integer coefficients, the rational roots theorem states that any rational roots of the polynomial must take the form

±factor of the constant termfactor of the leading coefficient.

For the given polynomial f(x), the constant term is −21 and the leading coefficient is 3. So, any rational roots of f(x) must take the form

±factor of −21factor of 3.

Therefore, the possible rational roots are as follows: ±1,±3,±7,±21,±13,±73.

Of these roots, the only root in the interval (−3,−2) is −73.

Now, let's use synthetic division to test whether (x+73) is a factor of f(x). We get the following:

Since the remainder is 0, we conclude that (x+73) is indeed a factor of f(x). Moreover, we have f(x)=(x+73)(3x2−9).

f(x)=(x+73)(3x2−9)=3(x+73)(x2−3)=3(x+73)(x+3)(x−3).

Setting the factors equal to 0, we find that, in ascending order, the roots of the polynomial are x=−73, x=−3, and x=3.
