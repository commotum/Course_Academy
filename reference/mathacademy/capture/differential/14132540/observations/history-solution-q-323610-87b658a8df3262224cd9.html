Given a polynomial with integer coefficients, the rational roots theorem states that any rational roots of the polynomial must take the form

±factor of the constant termfactor of the leading coefficient.

For the given polynomial f(x), the constant term is −8 and the leading coefficient is 1. So, any rational roots of f(x) must take the form

±factor of −8factor of 1.

Therefore, the possible rational roots are as follows: ±1,±2,±4,±8.

Of these roots, the only root in the interval (3,5) is 4.

Now, let's use synthetic division to test whether (x−4) is a factor of f(x). We get the following:

4 1 −1 −10 −8
↓ 4 12 8
1 3 2 0

Since the remainder is 0, we conclude that (x−4) is indeed a factor of f(x). Moreover, we have f(x)=(x−4)(x2+3x+2).

Since the highest-degree factor is now a quadratic, we can continue factoring using the usual methods:

f(x)=(x−4)(x2+3x+2)=(x−4)(x+1)(x+2).

Setting the factors equal to 0, we find that, in ascending order, the roots of the polynomial are x=−2, x=−1, and x=4.
