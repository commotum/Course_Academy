Let's denote by A and B the following statements:

  • A: You eat your vegetables.

  • B: You can have dessert.

Then, our initial statement can be written as A⇒B, which is logically equivalent to

¬A∨B.

The negation of A can be written as

  • ¬A: You don't eat your vegetables.

Therefore, the given statement is logically equivalent to the following:

You don't eat your vegetables, or you can have dessert.

We can write this in more everyday language as follows:

Either you don't eat your vegetables, or you can have dessert.

Note:

Using the word "Either" helps clarify that the sentence structure presents two alternatives and is particularly useful when employing the disjunctive form (¬P∨Q) in conversational or formal English. So, the phrase effectively conveys that one of the two stated conditions will hold true.
